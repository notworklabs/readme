Website: NotWorkLabs.com

Tagline: “It's Not Work If You Enjoy It.”

Version 1.0 | Concept, mission, culture, rules, product direction, and launch ideas

1. Executive Summary

NotWorkLabs is an open, community-driven digital makerspace for curious people. It brings together coworkers, colleagues, friends, students, hobbyists, makers, gamers, developers, tinkerers, and strangers around shared interests and voluntary participation.

The community is intentionally separate from employment, school obligations, and professional hierarchy. It is not another workplace, a productivity program, or a professional networking funnel. It is a place to explore personal projects, learn from one another, play games, share discoveries, and make interesting things together.

The guiding idea is simple: a multiplayer server for real life, combining the welcoming culture of a good gaming community, the collaborative spirit of open source, the experimentation of a hacker lab, and the creativity of a neighborhood makerspace.

At a glance

  • Name: NotWorkLabs
  • Website: NotWorkLabs.com
  • Tagline: It's Not Work If You Enjoy It.
  • Core promise: Curiosity is enough to belong.
  • Starting hub: Discord
  • Longer-term home: A website for projects, shared knowledge, events, and community showcases.
  • Audience: Anyone curious who wants to learn, share, play, build, experiment, or hang out.
  • Culture: Welcoming, family-friendly, community-first, voluntary, practical, and a little nerdy.

2. Project Definition

The concept

Create NotWorkLabs, an open, community-driven digital makerspace for curious people who want to learn, share, play, build, experiment, and have fun with like-minded people.

You do not need to be an expert, have an impressive project, work in technology, or know anyone to participate. Curiosity is enough.

NotWorkLabs is a place for personal projects, hobbies, experiments, interests, questions, and weird ideas. Members can join at their own pace and contribute in whatever way feels right to them.

What we encourage

Learn: Ask questions, explore unfamiliar subjects, teach yourself something new, and learn from others.

Build: Work on personal projects, software, hardware, electronics, art, Minecraft worlds, home labs, and creative experiments.

Share: Show what you are making, post discoveries, share resources, publish tutorials, and talk about what you have learned.

Play: Enjoy gaming, Minecraft, tabletop games, friendly competitions, and other shared activities.

Collaborate: Find people with complementary interests, form informal project groups, and make things together.

Experiment: Try new tools, explore emerging technologies, build prototypes, and embrace the occasional spectacular failure.

Have fun: Participate because you want to, not because someone expects you to.

Beginners should feel as welcome as experts. Asking a question is participation. Teaching someone is participation. Sharing a half-finished project is participation. Hanging out and playing games is participation.

Nobody needs to monetize a hobby, turn every project into a startup, or justify how they spend their free time.

3. Mission, Vision, and Values

Mission

Build an open, welcoming digital makerspace where people can turn curiosity into shared experiences, knowledge into capability, and ideas into things that exist.

Vision

A persistent online community where people can discover projects, find collaborators, learn skills, share knowledge, and enjoy making things together, whether they have five minutes or five hours to spare.

Values

  • Curiosity over credentials.
  • Collaboration over competition.
  • Experimentation over perfection.
  • Fun over obligation.
  • Generosity over gatekeeping.
  • Respect over status.
  • Privacy and consent by default.
  • Community health over growth at any cost.

4. Community Philosophy

NotWorkLabs should operate more like a well-run game server than a company, classroom, or professional networking group.

  • Everyone starts with the same basic expectations.
  • Experienced members help new players rather than gatekeeping.
  • People can participate at their own pace.
  • Collaboration is encouraged, but nobody is entitled to another person's time.
  • Community resources are shared responsibly.
  • Moderators maintain the environment rather than dictate what everyone thinks or creates.
  • Good-faith experimentation is encouraged, but deliberate disruption is not.
  • Nobody is required to be constantly active, productive, available, or impressive.
  • There are no mandatory meetings, performance reviews, productivity metrics, or expectations that members respond outside their availability.

This is a community, not a second job.

5. Community Rules

The rules should feel familiar to anyone who has spent time on a well-run game server: be decent, don't grief, respect other players, and help keep the space enjoyable.

Respect the Players

  • Treat everyone with basic respect, regardless of experience, background, interests, identity, or skill level.
  • Harassment, hate speech, discriminatory behavior, targeted abuse, threats, and deliberate intimidation are not welcome.
  • Disagreements happen. Discuss ideas without attacking people. Nobody has to agree with everyone, but everyone is expected to behave like a reasonable human being.

Keep It Family-Friendly

  • Keep the community safe for work and appropriate for a diverse, multigenerational audience.
  • No explicit sexual content, hateful imagery, offensive usernames or skins, or inappropriate builds and displays.
  • Political campaigning and deliberately inflammatory content do not belong in shared community spaces.
  • Friends and family, including minors where permitted by the platform and applicable rules, should be able to participate comfortably. Do not assume every member is an adult.

No Griefing

  • Respect other people's projects, creations, channels, servers, and contributions.
  • Do not destroy builds, sabotage projects, spam channels, disrupt events, steal work, or deliberately make the community worse for everyone else.
  • Experimentation is encouraged. Breaking things on purpose without permission is not the same as experimentation.

No Gatekeeping

  • You do not need credentials, a job title, expensive equipment, years of experience, or an existing project to belong here.
  • Help when you can. Ask when you need help. Share knowledge without making people feel stupid for not already knowing something.
  • Everyone was a beginner once.

Respect Privacy and Boundaries

  • Do not share private conversations, personal information, private project details, or another person's work outside its intended context without permission.
  • Do not pressure members to reveal real names, employers, locations, social accounts, or other personal details.
  • Respect people's time. A message is not a demand for an immediate response.

Keep Work at Work

  • NotWorkLabs is for personal interests and voluntary community participation, not workplace coordination.
  • Do not bring internal company business, confidential information, proprietary code, customer data, or nonpublic organizational details into the community.
  • Members may work together professionally, but participation here must remain separate from employment. Nobody should feel obligated to participate because of a reporting relationship or workplace hierarchy.

Share Responsibly

  • Credit creators, respect licenses, follow platform rules, and get permission before using someone else's work.
  • Do not upload malware, distribute stolen material, expose credentials, or use community projects to attack other people or systems.
  • Security research, home labs, and technical experimentation are welcome when conducted responsibly and with proper authorization.

No Spam or Unwanted Promotion

  • Sharing personal projects, useful resources, interesting discoveries, and relevant events is encouraged.
  • Repeated unsolicited advertising, referral spam, scams, manipulative recruitment, and aggressive self-promotion are not.
  • Contribute to the community. Do not treat its members as an audience to exploit.

Moderation Is About Keeping the Server Fun

  • Moderators are here to protect the community and its members, not to control what everyone thinks or builds.
  • Depending on the situation, moderation may involve a reminder, warning, content removal, temporary timeout, or removal from the community.
  • Serious misconduct can result in immediate removal. Decisions should be proportionate, consistent, and focused on maintaining a welcoming environment.

The Golden Rule

  • Don't be the reason someone stops enjoying the server.
  • If your behavior consistently makes the community less welcoming, less safe, or less fun for everyone else, this probably is not the right server for you.

Short version for onboarding

Be respectful. Keep it family-friendly. Don't grief. Don't gatekeep. Protect privacy. Keep work at work. Share responsibly. Don't spam. Respect the moderators. Help keep the server fun.

Rule of thumb: If you would not comfortably share it in a mixed-age community space, don't post it here.

6. The Culture We Want

NotWorkLabs should feel like logging into a great community server and discovering that someone has started a project, another person is teaching something interesting, a few people are gaming, and someone else has built something completely ridiculous.

There should always be room for the serious project, the silly experiment, the beginner's question, the late-night discovery, and the person who just wants to hang out.

We value curiosity over credentials, collaboration over competition, experimentation over perfection, and fun over obligation.

We want people to leave with new skills, new ideas, new friends, and the satisfaction of having made or learned something they genuinely care about.

7. Signature Community Ideas

1. Community Quests

Optional community challenges that give people a reason to try something new together.

  • Build Something Useless.
  • Automate One Annoying Thing.
  • Make a Tiny Game.
  • Learn Something and Teach It.
  • Build a Home Lab on a Budget.
  • Make Something Out of Spare Parts.
  • Recreate a Classic Game Mechanic.

Members can team up, share progress, and show off results. Keep it playful, accessible, and optional. Avoid leaderboards that turn participation into a grind.

2. Open Labs

Informal spaces for people exploring a shared interest. Possible labs include AI experiments, home labs, cybersecurity, 3D printing, electronics, coding, game development, creative writing, digital art, Minecraft, and self-hosting.

Each lab can have projects, resources, channels, and informal meetups without needing formal leadership or a bureaucratic structure.

3. Real-World Skill Trees

Members voluntarily share what they know and what they want to learn. Someone who knows Python might help another person build a first script, who later teaches someone else.

Progress is measured in skills acquired and knowledge shared, not points, status, or popularity. Skill trees should help people discover learning paths and potential mentors, not rank people by worth.

4. The Build Log

A community showcase for works in progress, experiments, failed attempts, lessons learned, and finished projects. Celebrate the process, not just polished results. A broken prototype with a good story belongs here just as much as a finished product.

5. Show-and-Tell Sessions

Optional, informal gatherings where members show a project, explain a discovery, demonstrate a tool, or share a lesson learned. Keep sessions short, welcoming, and interactive. No slide deck required.

6. Collaborative Side Quests

Small, low-pressure projects people can join for a short time: build a tiny open-source tool, create a Minecraft community build, document a home lab, design a sticker, or help someone debug a hobby project.

7. The Failure Museum

A playful place to share spectacular bugs, failed experiments, broken prototypes, and lessons learned. The point is to normalize experimentation and make failure useful rather than embarrassing.

8. Community Resource Cache

A shared collection of tutorials, project templates, starter kits, links, recommended tools, how-to guides, and member-created documentation. Keep resources useful, searchable, and easy to contribute to.

8. Product and Platform Direction

Phase 1: Discord as the starting hub

  • Welcome and start-here information.
  • Clearly visible community rules and reporting route.
  • Interest-based channels rather than a giant undifferentiated chat.
  • Project showcase and build-log channels.
  • Gaming and Minecraft channels where relevant.
  • Learning and help channels where beginner questions are encouraged.
  • Events, quests, and optional show-and-tell.
  • A place to share useful resources.
  • Moderation roles and a clear escalation path.

Possible channel layout

  • # welcome
  • # rules-and-how-this-works
  • # introductions (optional)
  • # general
  • # what-are-you-building
  • # build-log
  • # help-and-questions
  • # learning-resources
  • # community-quests
  • # show-and-tell
  • # gaming
  • # minecraft
  • # ai-and-experiments
  • # home-labs-and-self-hosting
  • # electronics-and-making
  • # events-and-hangouts
  • # off-topic

Treat this as a starting template, not a requirement to create every channel on day one. Start small and let actual member interests shape the server.

Phase 2: NotWorkLabs.com

The website can eventually become the persistent community directory and knowledge base, complementing Discord rather than duplicating every conversation.

  • Project directory: discover what members are building.
  • Member-contributed knowledge base: guides, tutorials, and lessons learned.
  • Community event board: quests, show-and-tell, workshops, and game nights.
  • Open Labs directory: find groups by interest.
  • Build Log: project updates and experiment write-ups.
  • Skill-tree or learning-path pages: discover skills and people willing to teach.
  • Resource Cache: curated links, templates, tools, and starter projects.
  • Community guidelines and transparent moderation information.

Product principle

Do not build another social network that competes for attention. Build a place where spending time online can result in something worthwhile: a friendship, a skill, a working prototype, a shared adventure, or simply a good time.

9. Design and Brand Direction

The brand should feel nerdy, playful, inventive, welcoming, and a little experimental. It should feel like a community workshop crossed with a multiplayer game server, not a corporate innovation lab.

Creative direction

  • Visual cues: workshop benches, pixel art, terminal windows, lab notebooks, schematics, crafting menus, maps, and playful interface details.
  • Tone: smart, friendly, curious, informal, and lightly irreverent.
  • Avoid: corporate stock imagery, startup hustle language, forced positivity, productivity dashboards, and aggressive gamification.
  • Accessibility: make the space easy to navigate for beginners and people with different experience levels.

Messaging examples

  • It's Not Work If You Enjoy It.
  • A multiplayer server for real life.
  • Curiosity is enough to join.
  • Build weird stuff. Learn cool things. Meet good people.
  • No experience required. No productivity reports.
  • Bring a project, a question, a game, or just yourself.

10. Community Operations and Guardrails

Keep participation voluntary

Avoid required attendance, daily streaks, quotas, mandatory introductions, contribution minimums, or pressure to reply quickly. People should be able to join, lurk, participate occasionally, take breaks, and return without explanation.

Keep the coworker boundary clear

The community may include coworkers, but it should not become a shadow workplace. Do not use managers' authority to recruit or pressure direct reports. Avoid internal work topics, company secrets, customer data, and proprietary work. Members should understand that participation is personal and optional.

Protect minors and mixed-age participation

Because the community is intended to be family-friendly and may include minors, use platform-appropriate safety controls, clear reporting paths, and moderation practices designed for mixed-age participation. Never imply that a family-friendly label alone makes every interaction safe.

Moderation basics

  • Publish clear rules before members join.
  • Provide an easy way to contact moderators or report issues.
  • Use consistent, proportionate enforcement.
  • Do not tolerate harassment, doxxing, threats, or malicious disruption.
  • Keep moderation decisions focused on behavior and community safety.
  • Avoid turning moderators into an unquestionable authority; provide a reasonable way to ask for clarification or appeal where appropriate.

Privacy and ownership

Encourage members to share only what they are comfortable making available to the community. Establish clear expectations for project ownership, licensing, reuse, and attribution. Do not assume that posting a project grants everyone permission to copy, redistribute, or commercialize it.

11. Suggested Launch Plan

Step 1: Establish the basics Secure the domain, publish a simple landing page, define the rules, and set up the Discord server with only the essential channels.

Step 2: Invite a small founding group Start with a mix of interests and experience levels. Invite people who are welcoming, curious, and likely to contribute positively, not just people who already know each other.

Step 3: Seed a few activities Run one optional community quest, one show-and-tell, and one casual gaming or maker hangout. Model the culture through actions rather than long announcements.

Step 4: Listen and adjust Ask what members want to learn, build, play, and share. Add channels and activities when there is real interest.

Step 5: Publish the first community projects Collect a few Build Logs, guides, or project showcases that demonstrate what the community is about.

Step 6: Grow through participation Invite people to bring a friend or share a useful project. Prioritize a healthy culture over rapid membership growth.

12. What Success Looks Like

Success should not be defined only by member count, message volume, or time spent online.

  • A beginner feels comfortable asking a question.
  • Members help one another without expecting payment or status.
  • People finish projects they are excited about.
  • Someone discovers a new hobby or skill.
  • A person finds collaborators for a personal project.
  • The community can host a fun event without making it feel mandatory.
  • Members trust that their boundaries and privacy will be respected.
  • People can take a break and return without guilt.
  • The space remains welcoming as it grows.

14. Open Decisions for the Founding Group

  • Will the Discord be open to anyone immediately, or begin as invite-based while moderation practices are established?
  • What minimum age and platform-specific safeguards will apply?
  • Will the website launch as a simple landing page or include project submissions from the start?
  • Which channels and interests are needed at launch, and which should wait for real demand?
  • Will community projects be open source by default, explicitly licensed, or left to each creator to decide?
  • Who will moderate, and how will reports, urgent issues, and appeals be handled?
  • What privacy and data-retention expectations should be communicated?
  • Will there be recurring quests or events, and how can they remain optional and low-pressure?

15. Closing Statement

NotWorkLabs is a place for people who like learning things, making things, breaking things in the name of science, playing games, sharing ideas, and meeting other curious humans.

Bring a project, a question, a game, an experiment, a skill to share, or just yourself. You do not need to be an expert, and you do not need to turn your hobby into a business.

Build weird stuff. Learn cool things. Meet good people.
